Overview

The short version

Deep drawers are dark, and small things hide at the back. So I made a drawer that turns its own light on when you pull it open and turns it off when you push it shut. It works the way a bat finds its way in the dark. A sensor sends out a sound too high to hear, listens for the echo, and uses the delay to work out how far away the drawer is.

This was an independent project completed in high school, and my first complete embedded system: a sensor, a decision and an actuated output, integrated into an enclosure of my own design. The drawer and its housing are original CAD from Autodesk Fusion, printed in two materials. The electronics comprise an Arduino, an HC-SR04 ultrasonic ranging module, a single LED and a 9 V battery.

Purpose, and where else the idea applies

The drawer is a compact instance of one of the most common patterns in engineering: acquire a measurement, compare it against a threshold, and act on the outcome. Here the measurement is distance, the threshold is 8 cm, and the action is illumination. Substitute any one of the three and the same circuit, with nearly identical firmware, addresses an entirely different application.

Cabinets, closets, toolboxes

Any enclosed storage accessed in poor light. There is no switch to locate and nothing to remember to turn off.

Parking and docking aids

Mounted on a garage wall, the sensor illuminates an indicator when the bumper reaches the target standoff.

Tank and bin level

Directed downward into a container, the sensor reports the distance to the surface, giving a non-contact fill level.

Touch-free triggers

A hand presented to the sensor can actuate a soap dispenser, a tap or a door, which matters in kitchens, laboratories and washrooms.

Engineering design process

  1. Define the problem

    Step 1 · Problem definition
    In plain words

    It is hard to find things in a dark drawer. I wanted the drawer to light itself.

    The user need is simple: see the contents of a drawer without holding a flashlight or fitting a switch. The illumination should require no deliberate action at all. Opening the drawer is the sole input.

  2. Set requirements and constraints

    Step 2 · Requirements
    In plain words

    I listed what the drawer had to do before choosing any parts.

    The light had to switch on automatically when the drawer opened and off when it closed. Detection had to be non-contact, with nothing that rubs, clicks or requires alignment on each cycle. The unit had to be self-contained and battery powered, with no wiring to the furniture or to mains. The output could not flicker with the drawer left partially open. And the trigger distance had to be a readily adjustable parameter, so the same design could be transferred to a different drawer.

  3. Choose how to sense the drawer

    Step 3 · Concept selection
    In plain words

    There are many ways to tell if a drawer is open. I picked the one that measures how far it has moved, not just whether it has moved.

    OptionPrincipleTrade-off
    Limit switchThe drawer depresses a contact when closed.Inexpensive, but a wearing mechanical contact that must be precisely aligned.
    Magnet and reed switchA magnet on the drawer closes a reed contact in the housing.Very low power, but reports only a binary open or closed state.
    Light sensorDetects ambient light entering the drawer.Fails in a dark room, which is precisely the operating condition of interest.
    Ultrasonic distance sensorTimes an acoustic echo from the rear of the drawer.Non-contact and yields a true distance. Draws more current than a switch.

    I selected the ultrasonic sensor. It satisfies the non-contact requirement, it turns the trigger point into a firmware constant rather than a bracket position, and a continuous distance measurement makes the design transferable to every application listed above. The cost is battery life, which is addressed in Step 7.

  4. Design the housing and the circuit

    Step 4 · Detailed design
    In plain words

    I drew the drawer and its sleeve on the computer, and I drew the wiring on paper before plugging anything in.

    The drawer and the outer sleeve were modelled in Autodesk Fusion and printed as separate parts, with a sliding clearance between them and a finger pull in the front face. The Arduino mounts to the rear of the sleeve, and the sensor faces forward toward the rear wall of the drawer.

    One sensor characteristic drove the mechanical layout. The HC-SR04 has a minimum range of approximately 2 cm, because the transducer is still ringing when a closer echo returns. The sensor is therefore recessed so that the closed drawer sits at roughly 3 cm, inside the valid measurement range.

    Electrically, the sensor requires four connections: 5 V, ground, a trigger input and an echo output. The LED is driven from a PWM-capable pin through a 220 Ω series resistor, limiting current to about 14 mA, within the 20 mA rating of an Arduino output. The 9 V battery supplies the Arduino's barrel jack, and the on-board linear regulator provides the 5 V rail for the sensor.

  5. Write the firmware

    Step 5 · Software
    In plain words

    The program asks "how far away is the drawer?" about sixteen times a second and switches the light to match.

    The control logic is a four-stage loop: measure, filter, decide, fade. The complete listing, annotated block by block, is in the next section.

  6. Test and tune

    Step 6 · Verification
    In plain words

    I watched the numbers the sensor was reporting, then picked the trigger distances from what I saw.

    Calibration came first. I streamed the measured distance to the serial monitor with the drawer closed and then open, and placed the two thresholds between those readings. The same exercise exposed the noise characteristics of an ultrasonic sensor: it periodically returns a spurious value when an echo is missed or reflects off a side wall. A median of the last three samples rejects a single outlier without adding perceptible latency.

    The final test held the drawer at the trigger point. With a single threshold the output chattered. With two thresholds separated by a dead band, it remained stable.

  7. Review the result

    Step 7 · Evaluation
    In plain words

    It works. The light comes on when the drawer opens. The battery is the weak point.

    The drawer meets every requirement in Step 2, and its principal limitation is power consumption. An Arduino Uno draws roughly 50 mA at idle, and a 9 V alkaline cell stores about 500 mAh, so continuous run time is on the order of ten hours. That is adequate for a demonstration and unacceptable for a product.

    A second revision would keep the microcontroller in a low-power sleep state for the majority of its life. A reed switch would wake it on drawer motion, it would take the ultrasonic measurement only then, and it would return to sleep once the drawer closed, reducing standby current from milliamps to microamps. A smaller board, such as a 3.3 V Pro Mini or a bare ATtiny, would remove the USB bridge and power LED that consume current to no purpose. For greater illumination, a short LED strip switched through a MOSFET would replace the single pin-driven LED.

The code, explained

Arduino C++ · no libraries needed · this is a cleaned-up and commented version of the sketch

// ---- Pin map -------------------------------------------------------------
const uint8_t TRIG_PIN = 9;
const uint8_t ECHO_PIN = 10;
const uint8_t LED_PIN  = 6;      // PWM pin so the light can fade

// ---- Tuning --------------------------------------------------------------
const float OPEN_CM   = 8.0;     // Farther than this = drawer is open
const float CLOSED_CM = 5.0;     // Closer than this  = drawer is closed
const unsigned long SAMPLE_MS   = 60;        // Time between sensor pings
const unsigned long ECHO_WAIT_US = 12000;    // Give up on an echo after 12 ms (about 2 m)
const unsigned long AUTO_OFF_MS = 120000;    // Light off after 2 min if drawer is left open
const uint8_t FADE_STEP = 15;                // Brightness change per sample
Part 1 of 5

Pins and tuning numbers

Every parameter that might require adjustment is declared by name at the top of the file. Adapting the unit to a deeper drawer changes only OPEN_CM and CLOSED_CM.

ECHO_WAIT_US is a timeout. If no echo arrives within 12 milliseconds the measurement is abandoned. Without it, a single lost echo would block execution for a full second.

// Fire one ultrasonic ping and return the distance in centimetres.
// Returns -1 if no echo came back in time.
float readDistanceCm() {
  digitalWrite(TRIG_PIN, LOW);
  delayMicroseconds(2);
  digitalWrite(TRIG_PIN, HIGH);
  delayMicroseconds(10);
  digitalWrite(TRIG_PIN, LOW);

  unsigned long echoUs = pulseIn(ECHO_PIN, HIGH, ECHO_WAIT_US);
  if (echoUs == 0) return -1;

  // Sound travels about 0.0343 cm per microsecond. The ping goes out and
  // comes back, so the one-way distance is half the round trip.
  return echoUs * 0.0343 / 2.0;
}
Part 2 of 5

Measuring distance with sound

A 10 microsecond pulse on TRIG commands the sensor to emit a burst of 40 kHz ultrasound. The sensor then holds ECHO high for the round-trip transit time, and pulseIn() measures that interval.

The speed of sound is approximately 343 m/s, or 0.0343 cm per microsecond. Because the pulse travels to the target and back, the range is half the product of transit time and speed.

// Middle value of three numbers. One bad reading cannot move the result.
float median3(float a, float b, float c) {
  if ((a >= b && a <= c) || (a <= b && a >= c)) return a;
  if ((b >= a && b <= c) || (b <= a && b >= c)) return b;
  return c;
}
Part 3 of 5

Median filter

The filter retains the middle value of the last three samples. Given readings of 3, 3 and 140, the result is 3, so one spurious echo is rejected outright. A mean of the same three values would return 49 and falsely trigger the light.

void setup() {
  pinMode(TRIG_PIN, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(ECHO_PIN, INPUT);
  pinMode(LED_PIN, OUTPUT);
  analogWrite(LED_PIN, 0);
}

void loop() {
  unsigned long nowMs = millis();
  if (nowMs - lastSample < SAMPLE_MS) return;
  lastSample = nowMs;
Part 4 of 5

Setup, and the timing of the loop

The loop samples every 60 ms. That interval allows the previous transmission to decay so the sensor does not detect its own residual echo. Timing uses millis() rather than delay(), which leaves the processor free should further features be added.

  // 1. Measure, and ignore pings that got no echo.
  float cm = readDistanceCm();
  if (cm > 0) {
    history[historyIndex] = cm;
    historyIndex = (historyIndex + 1) % 3;
  }
  float distance = median3(history[0], history[1], history[2]);

  // 2. Decide open or closed. Two thresholds with a gap between them
  //    (hysteresis) stop the light from flickering when the drawer sits
  //    right at the edge.
  if (!drawerOpen && distance > OPEN_CM) {
    drawerOpen = true;
    openedAt = nowMs;
  } else if (drawerOpen && distance > 0 && distance < CLOSED_CM) {
    drawerOpen = false;
  }

  // 3. Light on while open, unless it has been open for too long.
  bool wantLight = drawerOpen && (nowMs - openedAt < AUTO_OFF_MS);

  // 4. Fade toward the wanted state instead of snapping.
  if (wantLight && brightness < 255) {
    brightness = (brightness > 255 - FADE_STEP) ? 255 : brightness + FADE_STEP;
  } else if (!wantLight && brightness > 0) {
    brightness = (brightness < FADE_STEP) ? 0 : brightness - FADE_STEP;
  }
  analogWrite(LED_PIN, brightness);
}
Part 5 of 5

Measure, decide, fade

The light switches on above 8 cm but switches off only below 5 cm. That 3 cm dead band is hysteresis, the same mechanism a thermostat uses to prevent a furnace from cycling every few seconds.

If the drawer is left open for two minutes the light extinguishes to conserve the battery.

Brightness changes by 15 counts per sample, so the LED reaches full output in about one second. It is a small detail, but it makes the unit feel finished.

What this project taught me

Real sensors are noisy

The first revision trusted every sample. Filtering and hysteresis were the difference between a demonstration that mostly worked and one that always worked.

Know the sensor's limits

The 2 cm minimum range of the HC-SR04 dictated the sensor's mounting position. That was a mechanical decision driven by an electrical datasheet.

Power is a design requirement

I treated the battery as an afterthought. A current budget at the outset would have led directly to a lower-power architecture.
