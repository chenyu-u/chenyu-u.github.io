Ultrasonic Light‑Up Drawer

A self-lighting drawer that turns on when opened and off when closed. An Arduino measures the drawer's position with an HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor and fades a light-emitting diode on and off. I designed the drawer and housing in Autodesk Fusion computer-aided design software and produced them by fused deposition modelling 3D printing.

Overview

The short version

Deep drawers are dark, and small items get lost at the back. This drawer lights itself automatically when it is pulled open and switches off when it is pushed shut, with no switch to press. An ultrasonic sensor sends out a pulse of sound too high to hear and times the echo off the back of the drawer, which tells the Arduino how far open it is.

I designed and built this drawer as an independent project in high school. It was my first complete embedded system: a sensor, control logic and an output, all built into a custom enclosure. I modelled the drawer and housing in Autodesk Fusion computer-aided design software and printed them in two materials. The electronics are an Arduino, an HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor, a light-emitting diode and a 9 V battery. The page follows the project in the order I worked through it, from the problem to the finished drawer.

Purpose and applications

The drawer follows one of the most common patterns in engineering: take a measurement, compare it to a threshold and act on the result. Here the measurement is distance, the threshold is 8 cm and the action is lighting up. Change any one of the three and the same circuit, with almost the same code, does a completely different job.

Cabinets, closets, toolboxes

Any closed storage used in poor light, with no switch to find and nothing to remember to turn off.

Parking and docking aids

Mounted on a garage wall, it lights an indicator when a car's bumper reaches the right distance.

Tank and bin level

Pointed down into a container, it measures the distance to the surface and gives a fill level without touching the contents.

Touch-free triggers

A hand held in front of the sensor can trigger a soap dispenser, a tap or a door in kitchens, labs and washrooms.

Engineering design process

  1. Define the problem

    Step 1 · Problem definition
    In short

    A drawer that lights its own contents, with opening it as the only input.

    The need was simple: let users see inside a drawer without a flashlight or a switch. Turning on the light should take no deliberate action at all, so opening the drawer had to be the only input.

  2. Set requirements and constraints

    Step 2 · Requirements
    In short

    I listed what the drawer had to do before choosing any parts.

    • Automatic: the light turns on when the drawer opens and off when it closes.
    • Contactless: nothing that rubs, clicks or has to line up on every cycle.
    • Self-contained: battery powered, with no wiring to the furniture or a wall outlet.
    • Stable: no flicker when the drawer is left partly open.
    • Adaptable: the trigger distance is a simple setting, so the design fits other drawers.

  3. Choose how to sense the drawer

    Step 3 · Concept selection
    In short

    I compared four sensing methods and picked one that measures how far the drawer has opened, not just whether it is open.

    OptionPrincipleTrade-off
    Limit switchThe drawer presses a contact when it closes.Cheap, but the mechanical contact wears out and needs precise alignment.
    Magnet and reed switchA magnet on the drawer closes a reed switch in the housing.Very low power, but it can only tell open from closed.
    Light sensorDetects light entering the drawer.Does not work in a dark room, which is exactly when the light is needed.
    Ultrasonic distance sensorTimes an echo off the back of the drawer.Contactless and gives an actual distance, but uses more power than a switch.

    I selected the ultrasonic sensor. It met the contactless requirement, made the trigger point a setting in the firmware instead of a bracket position, and measured a real distance, so the same design carries over to every application above. The tradeoff was battery life, which I come back to in Step 7.

  4. Design the housing and the circuit

    Step 4 · Detailed design
    In short

    I designed the drawer and sleeve in computer-aided design software and planned the wiring on a schematic before assembly.

    I modelled the drawer and outer sleeve in Autodesk Fusion computer-aided design software and printed them as separate parts by fused deposition modelling, with a sliding clearance between them and a finger pull on the front. The Arduino mounts on the back of the sleeve, and the sensor faces forward toward the back wall of the drawer.

    One sensor limit set the layout. The HC-SR04 cannot measure anything closer than about 2 cm, because the transducer is still ringing when a very close echo returns. I set the sensor back in the housing so the closed drawer sits about 3 cm away, inside its working range.

    For the circuit design, the sensor needs four connections: 5 V, ground, a trigger input and an echo output. The light-emitting diode runs from a pulse-width modulation pin through a 220 Ω resistor, which limits the current to about 14 mA, within the 20 mA rating of an Arduino pin. The 9 V battery plugs into the Arduino's barrel jack, and the onboard regulator supplies 5 V to the sensor.

  5. Write the firmware

    Step 5 · Software
    In short

    About sixteen times a second, the firmware checks the drawer's position and sets the light to match.

    I wrote the Arduino C++ control loop in four stages: measure, filter, decide and fade. The full code, explained section by section, is in the next section.

  6. Test and tune

    Step 6 · Verification
    In short

    I calibrated the trigger distances from real sensor readings.

    I started with calibration. I streamed the measured distance to the serial monitor with the drawer closed and then open, and set the two thresholds between those readings. This also showed me how noisy an ultrasonic sensor is: it sometimes returned a wrong value when it missed an echo or caught one off a side wall. I added a median filter over the last three readings, which rejects a single bad value with no noticeable delay.

    For the final test I held the drawer right at the trigger point. With a single threshold the light flickered. With two thresholds and a gap between them (hysteresis), it stayed steady.

  7. Review the result

    Step 7 · Evaluation
    In short

    The finished drawer met every requirement, and battery life was the main area to improve.

    The finished drawer met every requirement from Step 2. Its main limitation was power. An Arduino Uno draws about 50 mA at idle and a 9 V alkaline battery holds about 500 mAh, so it ran for around ten hours. That was enough for a demonstration but not for everyday use.

    For a second version, I planned to keep the microcontroller asleep most of the time. A reed switch would wake it when the drawer moves, it would take ultrasonic readings only then, and it would go back to sleep once the drawer closed, cutting the standby current from milliamps to microamps. A smaller board such as a 3.3 V Pro Mini or a bare ATtiny would also remove the Universal Serial Bus chip and power light, which draw current for no reason. For brighter light, a light-emitting diode strip switched through a MOSFET would replace the single diode.

The code, explained

Arduino C++ · no libraries needed · this is a cleaned-up and commented version of the sketch

// ---- Pin map -------------------------------------------------------------
const uint8_t TRIG_PIN = 9;
const uint8_t ECHO_PIN = 10;
const uint8_t LED_PIN  = 6;      // pulse-width modulation pin so the light can fade

// ---- Tuning --------------------------------------------------------------
const float OPEN_CM   = 8.0;     // Farther than this = drawer is open
const float CLOSED_CM = 5.0;     // Closer than this  = drawer is closed
const unsigned long SAMPLE_MS   = 60;        // Time between sensor pings
const unsigned long ECHO_WAIT_US = 12000;    // Give up on an echo after 12 ms (about 2 m)
const unsigned long AUTO_OFF_MS = 120000;    // Light off after 2 min if drawer is left open
const uint8_t FADE_STEP = 15;                // Brightness change per sample
Part 1 of 5

Pins and tuning numbers

Every adjustable value is defined by name at the top of the file. Fitting the design to a deeper drawer only means changing OPEN_CM and CLOSED_CM.

ECHO_WAIT_US is a timeout. If no echo comes back within 12 milliseconds, the reading is dropped. Without it, one lost echo would freeze the program for a full second.

// Fire one ultrasonic ping and return the distance in centimetres.
// Returns -1 if no echo came back in time.
float readDistanceCm() {
  digitalWrite(TRIG_PIN, LOW);
  delayMicroseconds(2);
  digitalWrite(TRIG_PIN, HIGH);
  delayMicroseconds(10);
  digitalWrite(TRIG_PIN, LOW);

  unsigned long echoUs = pulseIn(ECHO_PIN, HIGH, ECHO_WAIT_US);
  if (echoUs == 0) return -1;

  // Sound travels about 0.0343 cm per microsecond. The ping goes out and
  // comes back, so the one-way distance is half the round trip.
  return echoUs * 0.0343 / 2.0;
}
Part 2 of 5

Measuring distance with sound

A 10 microsecond pulse on TRIG tells the sensor to send out a burst of 40 kHz ultrasound. The sensor then holds ECHO high for as long as the sound takes to travel out and back, and pulseIn() measures that time.

Sound travels at about 343 m/s, or 0.0343 cm per microsecond. Since the pulse goes to the drawer and back, the distance is half the travel time multiplied by the speed of sound.

// Middle value of three numbers. One bad reading cannot move the result.
float median3(float a, float b, float c) {
  if ((a >= b && a <= c) || (a <= b && a >= c)) return a;
  if ((b >= a && b <= c) || (b <= a && b >= c)) return b;
  return c;
}
Part 3 of 5

Median filter

The filter keeps the middle value of the last three readings. If the readings are 3, 3 and 140, the result is 3, so one bad echo is thrown out completely. An average of the same three values would give 49 and turn the light on by mistake.

void setup() {
  pinMode(TRIG_PIN, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(ECHO_PIN, INPUT);
  pinMode(LED_PIN, OUTPUT);
  analogWrite(LED_PIN, 0);
}

void loop() {
  unsigned long nowMs = millis();
  if (nowMs - lastSample < SAMPLE_MS) return;
  lastSample = nowMs;
Part 4 of 5

Setup, and the timing of the loop

The loop takes a reading every 60 ms, which gives the previous pulse time to die out so the sensor does not pick up its own leftover echo. Timing uses millis() instead of delay(), which leaves the processor free for more features later.

  // 1. Measure, and ignore pings that got no echo.
  float cm = readDistanceCm();
  if (cm > 0) {
    history[historyIndex] = cm;
    historyIndex = (historyIndex + 1) % 3;
  }
  float distance = median3(history[0], history[1], history[2]);

  // 2. Decide open or closed. Two thresholds with a gap between them
  //    (hysteresis) stop the light from flickering when the drawer sits
  //    right at the edge.
  if (!drawerOpen && distance > OPEN_CM) {
    drawerOpen = true;
    openedAt = nowMs;
  } else if (drawerOpen && distance > 0 && distance < CLOSED_CM) {
    drawerOpen = false;
  }

  // 3. Light on while open, unless it has been open for too long.
  bool wantLight = drawerOpen && (nowMs - openedAt < AUTO_OFF_MS);

  // 4. Fade toward the wanted state instead of snapping.
  if (wantLight && brightness < 255) {
    brightness = (brightness > 255 - FADE_STEP) ? 255 : brightness + FADE_STEP;
  } else if (!wantLight && brightness > 0) {
    brightness = (brightness < FADE_STEP) ? 0 : brightness - FADE_STEP;
  }
  analogWrite(LED_PIN, brightness);
}
Part 5 of 5

Measure, decide, fade

The light turns on above 8 cm but only turns off below 5 cm. That 3 cm gap is hysteresis, the same idea a thermostat uses to keep a furnace from switching on and off every few seconds.

If the drawer is left open for two minutes, the light turns off to save the battery.

Brightness changes by 15 steps per reading, so the light takes about a second to fade fully on. It is a small detail, but it makes the drawer feel finished.

What this project taught me

Real sensors are noisy

My first version trusted every reading. Adding filtering and hysteresis was the difference between a demo that mostly worked and one that always worked.

Know the sensor's limits

The HC-SR04's 2 cm minimum range decided where the sensor had to mount, so a line in an electrical datasheet ended up driving a mechanical decision.

Power is a design requirement

I treated the battery as an afterthought. Working out a current budget at the start would have led me straight to a lower-power design.
