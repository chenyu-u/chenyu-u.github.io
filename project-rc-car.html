Custom Bluetooth RC Car

Overview

The short version

I built a four-wheel-drive toy car from loose parts, and my phone is the controller. When I press a button on the phone, a small radio on the car hears it, an Arduino decides which wheels should spin, and a motor controller board sends battery power to the motors. The frame and the body were drawn on a computer and 3D printed.

This was a personal project in high school. I wanted to build a remote-controlled car from individual components rather than a kit, because a kit would have made every decision for me. Starting from loose parts meant I had to work out how the car was powered, how it steered, how the phone talked to it, and how all of that mounted onto a frame of my own design. The electronics are off-the-shelf modules, while the chassis plate, the motor mounts and the body shell are my own CAD, printed on an FDM printer.

System architecture

There are two paths through this car, and keeping them separate in my head made it far easier to wire and to debug. The control path carries information: a button press travels from the phone, through the radio, into the Arduino, and out to the motor controller. The power path carries energy, from the batteries through the switch to everything that needs electricity.

Engineering design process

  1. Define the problem

    Step 1 · Problem definition
    In plain words

    I wanted a car I could drive from my phone, built from parts I chose and a frame I drew myself.

    The goal was a phone-controlled car that drives forward, reverses and turns, with every electrical part carried on a chassis of my own design. A kit would have given me a working car in an afternoon and taught me very little. Starting from loose parts meant I had to understand why each part was there.

  2. Set requirements and constraints

    Step 2 · Requirements
    In plain words

    Before building anything, I wrote down what the car had to do and what I had to work with.

    The car had to drive forward and backward, turn left and right, and stop the moment nothing was pressed. For safety, it had to stop on its own if the phone disconnected, and a single switch had to cut all power. The body had to come off without tools so the wiring could be reached, and every structural part had to print on a desktop FDM printer without special supports.

    The biggest constraint was the parts I already had: 3 to 6 V gear motors, an L298N controller and 9 V batteries. The design had to work with those, and as you will see, that shaped several later decisions.

  3. Choose how it steers and how it is controlled

    Step 3 · Concept selection
    In plain words

    Instead of turning the front wheels like a real car, my car turns like a tank: one side goes forward while the other goes backward.

    A car-style layout needs a servo, a steering linkage and pivoting front hubs, all of which would have had to be printed accurately. Tank drive needs none of that. All four wheels are fixed, the two left motors are wired together, the two right motors are wired together, and the car turns on the speed difference between the sides. The cost is some tyre scrub in turns and no fine steering angle, but for a small indoor car that was a good trade, and it let all four motor mounts be the same part. It also meant a single L298N was enough, because each of its two channels drives one pair of motors.

    For the control link I used Bluetooth with the Dabble app. Dabble has a ready-made gamepad screen and an Arduino library that reports which buttons are held, so I could spend my time on how the car behaved instead of writing a phone app.

  4. Design the electrical system

    Step 4 · Electrical design
    In plain words

    The batteries push electricity to the motors, but the Arduino is the boss that decides when. The motor controller is a heavy-duty switch that the Arduino flips.

    The first thing I had to work out was the voltage budget. The L298N is an older bipolar design, and it loses roughly 2 V across its transistors. With a 9 V supply, that leaves about 7 V at the motors at full duty, which is above their 6 V rating. Rather than change the hardware, I handled it in firmware. The cruise speed is a PWM value of 170 out of 255, which averages to about 4.7 V at the motor, and the boost speed of 215 works out to about 6 V. The car never commands 100% duty.

    The two batteries are in parallel for a practical reason. A 9 V battery has high internal resistance, so its voltage drops sharply when four motors start at once. Two in parallel halve the current each one supplies and roughly double the run time, without raising the voltage. Everything shares one ground, because a control signal is only meaningful as a voltage relative to ground. If the grounds are not joined, the controller cannot read the Arduino's pins reliably.

    Wiring the motors in pairs also kept the pin count low. Each channel of the L298N needs three Arduino pins, one PWM pin for speed and two for direction, so the whole drive system uses six pins.

  5. Design and print the mechanical parts

    Step 5 · Mechanical design
    In plain words

    I measured the real parts, drew a frame around them on the computer, and printed it in plastic.

    I modelled the chassis in Autodesk Fusion as a flat plate, working from caliper measurements of the motors, the boards and the battery holders rather than dimensions from a product listing. The four motors mount underneath so the top surface stays free for electronics, and their leads come up through holes in the plate. The Arduino sits at the front, the batteries at the rear and the controller between them, which keeps the motor wires short and spreads the weight over both axles.

    The body is a separate shell with flat faces and shallow angles, so it prints cleanly without support material. It sits over the chassis and lifts straight off.

  6. Write the firmware

    Step 6 · Software
    In plain words

    The program is a loop that runs many times a second: check the phone, decide what each side should do, tell the motors.

    The firmware is short on purpose. It reads the gamepad, converts the button state into a target speed for each side, and then moves the real motor output toward that target in small steps. The full code, explained block by block, is in the next section.

  7. Integrate and test in stages

    Step 7 · Verification
    In plain words

    I tested one piece at a time, so when something went wrong I knew exactly which piece to blame.

    Power came first. I checked the switched rail and the 5 V pin with a multimeter before plugging in any module. Then, with the wheels off the ground, I commanded each side forward and swapped the two leads of any motor that spun the wrong way. Next came the radio on its own: pair the phone, open Dabble, and print the button states to the serial monitor before letting them drive anything. Only then did the car go on the floor, where I drove straight, reversed, spun both ways, and finally closed the app mid-drive to confirm that the car stopped.

  8. Review the result

    Step 8 · Evaluation
    In plain words

    The car drives and turns the way I wanted, and I also know exactly what I would do differently next time.

    The car meets every requirement I set in Step 2. Looking back at it with more experience, the first thing I would change is the battery chemistry. 9 V batteries are built for smoke detectors, not motors, and a two-cell lithium pack or six AA NiMH cells would deliver far more current with far less sag. The second is the motor controller. A MOSFET-based driver such as the TB6612FNG loses well under 0.5 V instead of 2 V, so much more of the battery's energy would reach the wheels.

    There are smaller improvements too. The Bluetooth module's RX pin is a 3.3 V input, so a two-resistor divider on the line from D3 is the correct way to drive it from a 5 V Arduino. The taped joints should be crimped connectors, with a fuse on the battery rail. And wheel encoders would let the firmware hold a straight line even when the four motors are not perfectly matched.

Part 1 of 5

Load the Dabble library

Dabble can do many things, from a gamepad to a terminal to sensor readouts. An Arduino Uno only has 32 KB of program memory, so these two #define lines tell the library to include the gamepad code and nothing else.

// ---- Pin map -------------------------------------------------------------
const uint8_t BT_RX_PIN = 2;
const uint8_t BT_TX_PIN = 3;

const uint8_t LEFT_EN   = 5;    // ENA: PWM speed, left pair
const uint8_t LEFT_FWD  = 4;
const uint8_t LEFT_REV  = 7;

const uint8_t RIGHT_EN  = 6;    // ENB: PWM speed, right pair
const uint8_t RIGHT_FWD = 8;
const uint8_t RIGHT_REV = 12;

// ---- Tuning --------------------------------------------------------------
const int SPEED_CRUISE = 170;   // 0 to 255. Default driving speed. About 4.7 V at the motors.
const int SPEED_BOOST  = 215;   // Held while the Triangle button is down. About 6 V at the motors.
const int SPEED_TURN   = 150;   // Spin-in-place speed. Lower is easier to control.
const int RAMP_STEP    = 12;    // Largest change in PWM per control cycle.
const unsigned long CYCLE_MS = 20;   // Control loop period (50 Hz).
Part 2 of 5

Name every pin and every tuning number

Every pin number and every speed lives at the top of the file with a name. If I move a wire or want a slower car, I change one line here and never touch the logic below.

SPEED_CRUISE = 170 is a PWM value. PWM switches the motor on and off very quickly, and at 170 out of 255 the motor is on two thirds of the time, which averages to about 4.7 V after the controller's losses.

// ---- State ---------------------------------------------------------------
int leftTarget = 0,  rightTarget = 0;    // What the driver is asking for, -255 to 255
int leftNow    = 0,  rightNow    = 0;    // What the motors are actually getting
unsigned long lastCycle = 0;

// Send one signed speed to one side of the car.
// Positive = forward, negative = reverse, zero = coast to a stop.
void driveSide(uint8_t enPin, uint8_t fwdPin, uint8_t revPin, int speed) {
  digitalWrite(fwdPin, speed > 0 ? HIGH : LOW);
  digitalWrite(revPin, speed < 0 ? HIGH : LOW);
  analogWrite(enPin, abs(speed));
}

// Move "now" toward "target" by at most RAMP_STEP so the motors never see a
// sudden jump from full forward to full reverse.
int ramp(int now, int target) {
  if (target > now) return min(now + RAMP_STEP, target);
  if (target < now) return max(now - RAMP_STEP, target);
  return now;
}
Part 3 of 5

Two small helper functions

driveSide() takes one signed number. Positive means forward, negative means reverse, and zero means stop. It sets the two direction pins to match the sign and writes the size of the number as the PWM speed. Because it takes the pins as inputs, the same function drives the left pair and the right pair.

ramp() limits how quickly the speed can change. Going from full forward straight to full reverse makes the motors draw a large current spike, which can pull the battery voltage low enough to reset the Arduino. Ramping spreads that change over about a third of a second.

void setup() {
  pinMode(LEFT_EN, OUTPUT);   pinMode(LEFT_FWD, OUTPUT);   pinMode(LEFT_REV, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(RIGHT_EN, OUTPUT);  pinMode(RIGHT_FWD, OUTPUT);  pinMode(RIGHT_REV, OUTPUT);
  driveSide(LEFT_EN,  LEFT_FWD,  LEFT_REV,  0);
  driveSide(RIGHT_EN, RIGHT_FWD, RIGHT_REV, 0);

  Dabble.begin(9600, BT_RX_PIN, BT_TX_PIN);   // Match the Bluetooth module's baud rate.
}
Part 4 of 5

Setup: runs once at power-on

All motor pins become outputs and both sides are commanded to zero before anything else happens, so the car cannot lurch when the switch is turned on. Then Bluetooth starts on pins 2 and 3 at 9600 baud, which has to match the module's setting.

void loop() {
  Dabble.processInput();    // Read whatever the phone has sent since last time.

  // 1. Turn button state into a target speed for each side.
  int speed = GamePad.isTrianglePressed() ? SPEED_BOOST : SPEED_CRUISE;

  if (!Dabble.isAppConnected()) {          // Phone out of range or app closed: stop.
    leftTarget = 0;             rightTarget = 0;
  } else if (GamePad.isUpPressed()) {
    leftTarget = speed;         rightTarget = speed;
  } else if (GamePad.isDownPressed()) {
    leftTarget = -speed;        rightTarget = -speed;
  } else if (GamePad.isLeftPressed()) {    // Left side backward, right side forward.
    leftTarget = -SPEED_TURN;   rightTarget = SPEED_TURN;
  } else if (GamePad.isRightPressed()) {
    leftTarget = SPEED_TURN;    rightTarget = -SPEED_TURN;
  } else {                                 // Nothing held: stop.
    leftTarget = 0;             rightTarget = 0;
  }

  // 2. Every 20 ms, step the real motor output toward the target.
  unsigned long nowMs = millis();
  if (nowMs - lastCycle >= CYCLE_MS) {
    lastCycle = nowMs;
    leftNow  = ramp(leftNow,  leftTarget);
    rightNow = ramp(rightNow, rightTarget);
    driveSide(LEFT_EN,  LEFT_FWD,  LEFT_REV,  leftNow);
    driveSide(RIGHT_EN, RIGHT_FWD, RIGHT_REV, rightNow);
  }
}
Part 5 of 5

Loop: runs forever

First it reads. Dabble.processInput() collects whatever the phone sent since the last pass.

Then it decides. The if chain turns the button state into a target for each side, and the very first check is the safety one: if the app is not connected, both targets are zero. Left and right turns run the sides in opposite directions so the car spins about its centre.

Finally it acts. Every 20 ms the real output takes one ramp step toward the target. This uses millis() instead of delay(), so the loop never stops listening to the phone while it waits.

What this project taught me

Signals and power are different problems

A logic pin carries information and a motor lead carries energy. Most of my early wiring mistakes came from treating them as the same thing.

Read the datasheet before picking the battery

The L298N's 2 V drop and the 9 V battery's internal resistance decided how this car performed, and both numbers were available before I bought anything.

Design around real parts

Measuring the actual motors and boards before modelling saved me reprints. Dimensions from a product listing are often wrong.

Test in stages

Power, then motors, then radio, then the whole car. Adding one new thing at a time makes every fault easy to find.
