Bluetooth-Controlled Car

A four-wheel-drive vehicle with a skid-steer drivetrain, operated from a mobile phone through the Dabble app. An Arduino Uno receives gamepad commands over a Bluetooth serial link and drives four direct-current gear motors through an L298N dual H-bridge motor driver. I designed the chassis, motor mounts and body shell in Autodesk Fusion computer-aided design software and manufactured them by fused deposition modelling 3D printing.

Overview

The short version

The vehicle is operated from a mobile phone through the Dabble gamepad interface. Commands are transmitted over Bluetooth to an Arduino microcontroller, which sets the speed and direction of each side of the drivetrain, and an L298N motor driver switches battery power to the four motors. Steering is achieved by driving the left and right sides at different speeds. The chassis and removable body shell are 3D printed components I designed in Autodesk Fusion.

I designed and built this vehicle as an independent project in high school. It integrates four engineering disciplines: circuit design and power distribution, embedded firmware, a wireless control link, and the mechanical design of the chassis that carries every subsystem. The electronics are commercial modules; the chassis plate, motor mounts and body shell are original designs I modelled in Autodesk Fusion computer-aided design software and manufactured by fused deposition modelling. This page follows the engineering design process in sequence, from problem definition through verification and evaluation of the completed vehicle.

Engineering design process

  1. Define the problem

    Step 1 · Problem definition
    In short

    A compact vehicle operated from a mobile phone, with every subsystem integrated on a custom chassis.

    The objective was a phone-operated vehicle capable of forward and reverse travel and turning in place, with the drivetrain, motor driver, wireless receiver and power supply all integrated on a chassis of my own design.

  2. Set requirements and constraints

    Step 2 · Requirements
    In short

    I defined the functional, safety and manufacturing requirements before beginning any design work.

    • Function: forward and reverse travel, left and right turns, and a stop whenever no command is held.
    • Safety: an automatic stop if the Bluetooth link is lost, and a single switch that isolates all power.
    • Serviceability: a body shell that removes without tools, keeping the wiring accessible.
    • Manufacturability: every structural component prints on a desktop fused deposition modelling printer without support material.

    The design was also constrained to a fixed parts inventory: 3 to 6 V gear motors, an L298N motor driver and 9 V batteries. As the following steps show, these components drove several later design decisions.

  3. Select the steering, control and system architecture

    Step 3 · Concept selection
    In short

    I selected skid steering, in which the two sides of the drivetrain run at different speeds or in opposite directions to turn, and built the system architecture around it.

    Ackermann (car-style) steering would have required a servo, a steering linkage and pivoting front hubs, all with tolerances that are difficult to hold in printed parts. I selected differential (skid) steering instead, which eliminates the steering mechanism entirely. All four wheels are fixed, the two motors on each side are wired in parallel, and the vehicle turns by driving the two sides at different speeds. The tradeoffs are tyre scrub during turns and the absence of a proportional steering angle, neither of which is significant for a small indoor vehicle. The layout also allowed all four motor mounts to share one common part, and a single L298N to drive the complete drivetrain, with one channel per side.

    For the control interface I selected Dabble, a mobile app with a ready-made gamepad interface and an Arduino library that reads button states directly. This kept development effort focused on vehicle behaviour rather than on building a custom phone application.

    With the concept selected, I defined the system architecture as two independent paths. The signal path carries information: a gamepad input in the Dabble app is transmitted over the Bluetooth link, decoded by the Arduino, and passed to the motor driver as logic-level signals. The power path carries energy from the batteries, through the main isolation switch, to the motor driver and the Arduino. Designing the two paths separately simplified the wiring harness and made faults straightforward to isolate during testing.

  4. Design the electrical system

    Step 4 · Electrical design
    In short

    The batteries supply power, the Arduino determines when each motor runs, and the motor driver performs the high-current switching.

    I began the circuit design with a voltage budget. The L298N drops about 2 V across its output transistors under load, so a 9 V supply leaves about 7 V at the motors at full duty cycle, which exceeds their 6 V rating. Rather than change the hardware, I limited the duty cycle in firmware. The cruise setting of 170 out of 255 delivers an average of about 4.7 V at the motors and the boost setting of 215 delivers about 6 V, so the motors are never driven at 100% duty.

    I wired the two batteries in parallel because a 9 V alkaline battery has a high internal resistance, and its terminal voltage drops sharply when four motors draw current simultaneously. In parallel, each battery supplies half the load current and the run time roughly doubles, with no increase in supply voltage. All subsystems share a common ground reference, so the motor driver always reads the Arduino's logic levels correctly.

    Wiring the motors in pairs minimised the pin count. Each L298N channel uses three Arduino pins: one pulse-width modulation output for speed and two digital outputs for direction, so the complete drivetrain uses six pins.

  5. Design and manufacture the mechanical components

    Step 5 · Mechanical design
    In short

    I designed a custom chassis and body shell around the measured components and manufactured them by 3D printing.

    I modelled the chassis in Autodesk Fusion computer-aided design software as a flat plate, dimensioned from caliper measurements of the motors, circuit boards and battery holders rather than from product listings. The four motors mount to the underside of the plate, leaving the upper surface clear for the electronics, with the motor leads routed through holes in the plate. The Arduino sits at the front, the batteries at the rear and the motor driver between them, which keeps the motor leads short and distributes the mass over both axles.

    The body is a separate shell with flat faces and shallow draft angles, so it prints without supports. It locates over the chassis and lifts straight off. I manufactured every part by fused deposition modelling 3D printing, then assembled the vehicle and soldered the electronics on the chassis.

  6. Develop the firmware

    Step 6 · Software
    In short

    A fixed-rate control loop reads the controller input, sets a target for each side and updates the motor outputs.

    I kept the Arduino C++ firmware compact. It reads the Dabble gamepad, converts the button states into a target speed and direction for each side of the drivetrain, and ramps the motor output toward that target in fixed increments for smooth acceleration. The complete source, explained section by section, is in the next section.

  7. Integrate and test in stages

    Step 7 · Verification
    In short

    I verified each subsystem independently before testing the assembled vehicle.

    I began with the power system, using a multimeter to verify the switched rail and the 5 V regulator output before connecting any module. Next, with the wheels raised off the ground, I drove each side forward and reversed the leads at the screw terminal of any motor that turned in the wrong direction. I then tested the Bluetooth link in isolation by pairing the phone, opening Dabble and printing the button states to the serial monitor. Only then did the vehicle proceed to floor testing for straight-line travel, reverse and turning. The final test closed the app mid-run to confirm that the fail-safe stopped the vehicle.

  8. Evaluate the result

    Step 8 · Evaluation
    In short

    The completed vehicle met every requirement, and the evaluation identified clear improvements for a second revision.

    The completed vehicle met every requirement defined in Step 2. The first improvement the evaluation identified was the power source. 9 V alkaline batteries are designed for low-drain devices such as smoke detectors, not motor loads; a two-cell lithium-ion pack or six nickel-metal hydride AA cells would deliver far higher current with much less voltage sag. The second was the motor driver. A MOSFET-based driver such as the TB6612FNG drops well under 0.5 V rather than 2 V, so considerably more of the battery's energy would reach the wheels.

    I also identified secondary improvements. A resistor divider on the Bluetooth RX line would protect its 3.3 V input from the Arduino's 5 V signal on D3. Crimped connectors would replace the taped joints, a fuse would protect the battery rail, and wheel encoders would enable closed-loop speed control, so the vehicle could hold a straight line despite small speed differences between motors.

Part 1 of 5

Include the Dabble library

Dabble provides several modules, including a gamepad, a terminal and sensor readouts. An Arduino Uno has only 32 KB of program memory, so these two #define directives instruct the library to compile only the gamepad module.

// ---- Pin map -------------------------------------------------------------
const uint8_t BT_RX_PIN = 2;
const uint8_t BT_TX_PIN = 3;

const uint8_t LEFT_EN   = 5;    // ENA: pulse-width modulation speed, left pair
const uint8_t LEFT_FWD  = 4;
const uint8_t LEFT_REV  = 7;

const uint8_t RIGHT_EN  = 6;    // ENB: pulse-width modulation speed, right pair
const uint8_t RIGHT_FWD = 8;
const uint8_t RIGHT_REV = 12;

// ---- Tuning --------------------------------------------------------------
const int SPEED_CRUISE = 170;   // 0 to 255. Default driving speed. About 4.7 V at the motors.
const int SPEED_BOOST  = 215;   // Held while the Triangle button is down. About 6 V at the motors.
const int SPEED_TURN   = 150;   // Spin-in-place speed. Lower is easier to control.
const int RAMP_STEP    = 12;    // Largest change in pulse-width modulation per control cycle.
const unsigned long CYCLE_MS = 20;   // Control loop period (50 Hz).
Part 2 of 5

Define every pin and tuning constant

Every pin assignment and tuning value is defined once, by name, at the top of the file. Reassigning a wire or adjusting a speed is a one-line change that never touches the control logic.

SPEED_CRUISE = 170 is a pulse-width modulation duty value. Pulse-width modulation switches the motor supply on and off at high frequency, and a duty of 170 out of 255 energises the motor about two thirds of the time. After the driver's losses, this averages about 4.7 V at the motors.

// ---- State ---------------------------------------------------------------
int leftTarget = 0,  rightTarget = 0;    // What the driver is asking for, -255 to 255
int leftNow    = 0,  rightNow    = 0;    // What the motors are actually getting
unsigned long lastCycle = 0;

// Send one signed speed to one side of the drivetrain.
// Positive = forward, negative = reverse, zero = coast to a stop.
void driveSide(uint8_t enPin, uint8_t fwdPin, uint8_t revPin, int speed) {
  digitalWrite(fwdPin, speed > 0 ? HIGH : LOW);
  digitalWrite(revPin, speed < 0 ? HIGH : LOW);
  analogWrite(enPin, abs(speed));
}

// Move "now" toward "target" by at most RAMP_STEP so the motors never see a
// sudden jump from full forward to full reverse.
int ramp(int now, int target) {
  if (target > now) return min(now + RAMP_STEP, target);
  if (target < now) return max(now - RAMP_STEP, target);
  return now;
}
Part 3 of 5

Two helper functions

driveSide() accepts one signed value. A positive value drives the pair forward, a negative value drives it in reverse, and zero stops it. The function sets the two direction pins from the sign and writes the magnitude as the pulse-width modulation duty. Because the pin numbers are passed as arguments, one function serves both sides of the drivetrain.

ramp() limits the rate at which the duty can change. A step from full forward to full reverse produces a current spike that can pull the supply voltage low enough to reset the Arduino. Ramping spreads that transition over roughly a third of a second.

void setup() {
  pinMode(LEFT_EN, OUTPUT);   pinMode(LEFT_FWD, OUTPUT);   pinMode(LEFT_REV, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(RIGHT_EN, OUTPUT);  pinMode(RIGHT_FWD, OUTPUT);  pinMode(RIGHT_REV, OUTPUT);
  driveSide(LEFT_EN,  LEFT_FWD,  LEFT_REV,  0);
  driveSide(RIGHT_EN, RIGHT_FWD, RIGHT_REV, 0);

  Dabble.begin(9600, BT_RX_PIN, BT_TX_PIN);   // Match the Bluetooth module's baud rate.
}
Part 4 of 5

Setup: runs once at power-on

All motor pins are configured as outputs and both sides are commanded to zero before anything else executes, so the vehicle remains stationary at power-up. The Bluetooth link then initialises on pins 2 and 3 at 9600 baud, matching the module's configured rate.

void loop() {
  Dabble.processInput();    // Read whatever the phone has sent since last time.

  // 1. Turn button state into a target speed for each side.
  int speed = GamePad.isTrianglePressed() ? SPEED_BOOST : SPEED_CRUISE;

  if (!Dabble.isAppConnected()) {          // Phone out of range or app closed: stop.
    leftTarget = 0;             rightTarget = 0;
  } else if (GamePad.isUpPressed()) {
    leftTarget = speed;         rightTarget = speed;
  } else if (GamePad.isDownPressed()) {
    leftTarget = -speed;        rightTarget = -speed;
  } else if (GamePad.isLeftPressed()) {    // Left side backward, right side forward.
    leftTarget = -SPEED_TURN;   rightTarget = SPEED_TURN;
  } else if (GamePad.isRightPressed()) {
    leftTarget = SPEED_TURN;    rightTarget = -SPEED_TURN;
  } else {                                 // Nothing held: stop.
    leftTarget = 0;             rightTarget = 0;
  }

  // 2. Every 20 ms, step the real motor output toward the target.
  unsigned long nowMs = millis();
  if (nowMs - lastCycle >= CYCLE_MS) {
    lastCycle = nowMs;
    leftNow  = ramp(leftNow,  leftTarget);
    rightNow = ramp(rightNow, rightTarget);
    driveSide(LEFT_EN,  LEFT_FWD,  LEFT_REV,  leftNow);
    driveSide(RIGHT_EN, RIGHT_FWD, RIGHT_REV, rightNow);
  }
}
Part 5 of 5

Main loop: runs continuously

First, the loop reads input. Dabble.processInput() retrieves any data the phone has sent since the previous pass.

Next, it decides. A chain of conditional statements maps the button states to a target speed for each side. The first check is the fail-safe: if the app is not connected, both targets are set to zero. For left and right turns, the two sides drive in opposite directions so the vehicle pivots in place.

Finally, it acts. Every 20 ms the output advances one ramp step toward the target. Timing uses millis() rather than delay(), so the loop continues to service the Bluetooth link between updates.

Key lessons

Signal and power are separate design problems

A logic pin carries information; a motor lead carries power. Most of my early wiring faults came from treating the two alike, so I learned to design and route them as separate paths.

Consult the datasheet before selecting components

The L298N's 2 V drop and the 9 V battery's internal resistance set the performance limits of this vehicle, and both figures were in the datasheets before I purchased anything.

Design around measured components

Modelling the chassis from caliper measurements of the actual motors and boards avoided reprints, because the dimensions in product listings were often inaccurate.

Verify in stages

Bringing up the power system, then the motors, then the wireless link, then the complete vehicle meant that every fault was straightforward to isolate.
