Overview

The short version

Researchers wanted to know how people feel inside their apartments during hot summers. Are they too warm, is the air stuffy, are they tired or stressed? Asking once at the end of the day does not work, because people forget. So I built a watch face that buzzes four times a day and asks a handful of questions with big picture buttons. Each answer travels from the watch to a phone, from the phone to the internet, and finally into a spreadsheet the research team can study.

I developed this application during my summer research position with the Building Energy and Indoor Environment (BEIE) Lab, part of the Hub for Advancing Buildings at the University of Toronto. The study belongs to a PhD candidate in the lab and employs ecological momentary assessment (EMA), a method in which participants answer brief surveys in the moment and in the environment under study. The hardware platform, a Fitbit Versa 2 paired with a dedicated Android phone in each participant kit, was fixed before I joined. My scope was the software: the clock face on the watch, the companion application on the phone, the data pipeline downstream of both, and the documentation required for the lab to rebuild and maintain the system independently.

System architecture

The system is partitioned into four layers, each with a single responsibility and a defined hand-off to the next. The partitioning is deliberate: when a fault occurs, it can be attributed to one layer.

LayerPlatformResponsibility
Watch applicationFitbit OS, SDK 4.2Renders the clock face and the SURVEY control, maintains the schedule, presents one question at a time, and transmits completed responses to the phone.
CompanionFitbit mobile app on AndroidAssembles a flat session record, stamps it with the participant ID and a local timestamp, writes a local CSV backup, uploads it, and retries on failure.

Engineering design process

  1. Define the problem

    Step 1 · Problem definition
    In plain words

    The lab needed a way to ask people how they feel at home, several times a day, without a researcher being there.

    The study examines how residents of multi-unit residential buildings experience summer heat. That requires self-reported comfort and wellbeing data captured several times a day across an entire season, from participants going about their normal routines, and delivered as clean, analysis-ready records with no manual transcription.

  2. Set requirements and constraints

    Step 2 · Requirements
    In plain words

    Before writing any code, I wrote down what the survey had to do and the limits I had to work inside.

    The application had to prompt participants at 9:00, 12:00, 15:00 and 18:00, and had to allow a participant-initiated survey at any time of thermal discomfort. Each survey had to fit a 300 by 300 pixel display and be completed in a few taps with no text entry, using large icons and colour cues rather than small type. On privacy, the application could record survey responses only, with no heart rate, activity or other Fitbit health data, and every record had to be keyed to a study identifier rather than an account.

    Several operational constraints applied. A response could not be lost because the phone was momentarily offline. The storage service had to operate within a free tier, and the request budget confirmed it would: twenty kits, four surveys a day and two requests per survey is about 160 requests a day, against a limit of roughly 10,000 a month. The platform was also fixed. A Versa 2 mandates Fitbit SDK 4.2, which in turn mandates an older JavaScript runtime on the watch and Node 14 for the toolchain.

  3. Make the key design decisions

    Step 3 · Concept selection
    In plain words

    I made the survey the watch face itself, so it is already on screen the moment someone looks at their wrist.

    The first decision was the application type. A Fitbit app must be launched from a menu, whereas a clock face is persistently displayed. I registered the project as a clock face, so the survey is present the moment the participant raises their wrist, and the device continues to function as a watch between surveys.

    The second decision concerned the user interface architecture. Rather than authoring a discrete screen for each question, I built a single template with a two-option row and a three-option row, and defined every question as an entry in a configuration array. The rendering code populates text, icons and colours from that array, so adding or rewording a question is a one-object edit. That mattered, because the question set changed during development.

    The third decision was data persistence. The companion can issue ordinary HTTPS requests, so a hosted JSON store was sufficient and removed the need to provision, secure and fund a server for a student-built tool.

  4. Design the survey logic and the data record

    Step 4 · Detailed design
    In plain words

    I drew the path a survey takes as a flowchart first, so every possible ending was decided before I wrote code.

    The first question establishes whether the participant is at home. Because the study concerns conditions inside the apartment, a negative response terminates the survey immediately and stores an abbreviated record. That minimises participant burden while still confirming that the prompt was received.

    Every session is stored as one flat record with a fixed schema of fourteen fields. A question that was not presented is stored as null and serialises to an empty cell in the CSV. A constant schema means the analysis scripts never have to infer which columns are present.

    Timestamps required specific handling. JavaScript's native timestamp is expressed in UTC, while the survey schedule is defined in the participant's local time. I implemented a helper that records local time with its UTC offset, for example 2025-09-01T09:04:22-04:00, so a 9:00 survey is recorded as 9:00.

  5. Build within the platform limits

    Step 5 · Implementation
    In plain words

    The watch is a tiny, older computer. Some things that work on a laptop do not work on it, and I had to find out which.

    The SDK 4.2 runtime on the watch does not support arrow functions, so every handler in the watch code is a named function declaration; the companion executes in a newer engine on the phone and carries no such restriction. Touch handling also required adjustment: click events proved unreliable on the device, whereas a mousedown event bound to a transparent rectangle over each control registered consistently.

    The icon set presented a rendering problem. The artwork was dark on a transparent background and was invisible against the black display. I placed a coloured tile behind each icon, and the tile colour became an interface element in its own right: green for comfortable, red and blue for the two opposing directions, and yellow for neutral. I also found that an image element declared without an initial source could fail to initialise and would then reject a source assigned later, so every image in the layout now carries a valid placeholder.

  6. Test, find faults, fix them

    Step 6 · Verification
    In plain words

    I used the watch the way a real participant would, including ignoring it, and fixed the problems that only show up that way.

    The most serious defect surfaced when a survey was ignored. The next timer was armed only on survey completion, and an unanswered survey never completes, so every subsequent survey failed to fire. The correction arms the next slot at the start of each survey, before the first question is displayed. A related defect left the manual-trigger flag set after an ignored manual survey, mislabelling the next scheduled session; the timer callback now clears the flag before starting.

    The sleep question was bound to the 9:00 slot, so participants absent in the morning were never asked. It is now inserted after the first affirmative home response of the day, and its date stamp is recorded only when it is actually displayed. On the companion, an upload was originally attempted once, so a session was lost if the phone was offline at that moment. The companion now writes a local CSV first, retains the session in a buffer, and retries on every reconnection. One further fault was environmental rather than software: the university network blocks the developer bridge between the laptop and the phone, which prevented installation to the watch on campus. Sideloading over a phone hotspot on the 2.4 GHz band, the only band the Versa 2 supports, resolved it.

  7. Verify on hardware and hand over

    Step 7 · Handover
    In plain words

    I proved the app worked on a real watch, then wrote instructions so the person running the study could load the rest without me.

    My scope was the development of the clock face and companion, so I installed and verified the application on a single test watch. The verification is end to end: complete a survey on the device, observe the confirmation screen, and confirm that the record appears in the cloud store under the correct participant ID. The PhD candidate who owns the study sideloaded the remaining watches and deployed the kits to participants.

    To support that handover, I wrote a 24-section development guide covering the architecture, the build environment, the sideloading procedure, the data schema, the verification procedure and every known failure mode with its remedy. Research software is frequently lost when its author graduates. The guide exists so that this system is not.

  8. Review the result

    Step 8 · Evaluation
    In plain words

    It does the job. I also know where its weak spots are, and I wrote them down.

    The application supports a multi-summer study with more than 20 participants and delivers a CSV the research team can analyse directly. Its limitations are documented for the lab. Timers are held in memory, so after a device restart the schedule resumes only when the clock face reloads, because Fitbit OS provides a clock face with no persistent alarm. The watch does not buffer, so a session completed while the phone is out of Bluetooth range is lost, as buffering exists on the phone only.

    The final limitation is more subtle. Each upload reads the entire array, appends one record and writes the array back, and that read-modify-write sequence is not atomic. Two phones uploading within the same second could overwrite one another. At 160 uploads a day the probability is low and the local CSV backup provides recovery, but the correct remedy is an append-only endpoint or a separate store per participant.

A question is data, not code

Every question is one object of this form. The rendering code never references temperature or humidity; it only knows how to draw the object it is given.

The value is the stored datum, the label is the text presented to the participant, and the color is the tile rendered behind the icon.

Watch app

Schedule the next survey first

This ordering is the correction for the most serious defect found in testing. Because scheduleSurvey() executes before the first question is displayed, the 12:00 survey is armed even if the 9:00 survey is never answered.

The sleep question is filtered out at the start and reinserted later, conditional on the participant confirming they are home. The three vibrations are spaced 600 ms apart so the prompt is difficult to miss.

Phone companion

Never lose an answer

When responses arrive from the watch, three operations execute in a fixed order: local backup, insertion into the retry buffer, then upload. A session leaves the buffer only after the server acknowledges it.

The second handler runs on every reconnection of the watch and retries any session still pending.

What this project taught me

Test the unhappy path

The most serious defect appeared only when a survey was ignored, which is exactly what real participants do. Testing nominal behaviour alone would have missed it.

Design the data first

Fixing the record schema early allowed the watch, the phone and the retrieval script to be developed and revised independently.

Documentation is part of the deliverable

In a research lab, the next person to maintain the code is another student. Software that cannot be rebuilt is not a deliverable.

Be explicit about limits

Every system has failure modes. Documenting them, with the conditions that trigger them, is more valuable than omitting them.
